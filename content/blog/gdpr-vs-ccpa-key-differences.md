<p>If you operate a website that serves users in Europe or California, you've likely heard of the <strong>GDPR</strong> (General Data Protection Regulation) and the <strong>CCPA</strong> (California Consumer Privacy Act). Both aim to protect consumer privacy, but they differ in scope, enforcement, and requirements. Understanding these differences is crucial for compliance.</p>

      <h3>What is the GDPR?</h3>
      <p>The GDPR is a comprehensive privacy regulation enacted by the European Union in May 2018. It applies to any organization that processes personal data of individuals located in the EU, regardless of where the organization is based. The GDPR is widely considered the gold standard of data privacy legislation.</p>
      <p>Key features of the GDPR include:</p>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Consent-based processing:</strong> Organizations must obtain explicit, informed consent before collecting personal data.</li>
        <li><strong>Right to erasure:</strong> Individuals can request that their personal data be permanently deleted.</li>
        <li><strong>Data portability:</strong> Users have the right to receive their data in a machine-readable format and transfer it to another service.</li>
        <li><strong>Data Protection Officers:</strong> Some organizations are required to appoint a dedicated data protection officer.</li>
        <li><strong>72-hour breach notification:</strong> Organizations must notify authorities of data breaches within 72 hours.</li>
      </ul>

      <h3>What is the CCPA (and CPRA)?</h3>
      <p>The CCPA is a state-level privacy law in California, effective since January 2020. It was significantly expanded by the <strong>California Privacy Rights Act (CPRA)</strong>, which took effect January 1, 2023. The law applies to for-profit businesses that meet certain thresholds: annual gross revenue exceeding $26.6 million (adjusted annually for inflation), or processing personal information of 100,000 or more consumers or households annually.</p>
      <p>Key features of the CCPA/CPRA include:</p>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Right to know:</strong> Consumers can request disclosure of what personal information a business collects about them — with no 12-month look-back limit as of 2026.</li>
        <li><strong>Right to delete:</strong> Similar to the GDPR's right to erasure.</li>
        <li><strong>Right to correct:</strong> Added by the CPRA — consumers can request correction of inaccurate personal information.</li>
        <li><strong>Right to opt-out:</strong> Consumers can opt out of having their personal information sold or shared with third parties.</li>
        <li><strong>Sensitive personal information:</strong> The CPRA created a new protected category covering financial credentials, racial/ethnic origin, health data, and more, with heightened protections.</li>
        <li><strong>Non-discrimination:</strong> Businesses cannot discriminate against consumers who exercise their CCPA/CPRA rights.</li>
        <li><strong>2026 updates:</strong> New CPPA regulations effective January 1, 2026 added requirements for automated decision-making technology (ADMT), cybersecurity audits, and the DELETE, Request, and Opt-Out (DROP) platform for data broker opt-outs.</li>
      </ul>

      <h3>Side-by-Side Comparison</h3>
      <p>Here are the major differences between the two regulations:</p>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Geographic scope:</strong> GDPR applies to anyone processing EU residents' data. CCPA applies only to businesses meeting specific thresholds that serve California residents.</li>
        <li><strong>Consent model:</strong> GDPR requires <em>opt-in</em> consent before data collection. CCPA/CPRA uses an <em>opt-out</em> model — businesses can collect data by default but must allow consumers to opt out of its sale or sharing.</li>
        <li><strong>Definition of personal data:</strong> GDPR covers any data that can identify a person. CCPA covers personal information that "identifies, relates to, or could reasonably be linked" to a consumer or household, with CPRA adding an expanded "sensitive personal information" subcategory.</li>
        <li><strong>Penalties:</strong> GDPR fines can reach up to €20 million or 4% of global annual revenue (whichever is greater). CCPA/CPRA fines are up to $7,500 per intentional violation, with lower-tier GDPR violations capped at €10 million or 2% of global revenue.</li>
        <li><strong>Private right of action:</strong> CCPA/CPRA allows consumers to sue businesses directly after certain data breaches. GDPR enforcement is primarily through national supervisory authorities.</li>
        <li><strong>Enforcement bodies:</strong> GDPR is enforced by EU data protection authorities. California created the California Privacy Protection Agency (CPPA) to enforce CCPA/CPRA — the first dedicated state privacy regulator in the US.</li>
      </ul>

      <h3>Do You Need to Comply with Both?</h3>
      <p>If your website serves users in both the EU and California, yes — you need to comply with both regulations. The simplest approach is to implement the stricter standard (usually GDPR) across your entire site, which will generally satisfy CCPA/CPRA requirements as well. As of 2026, <strong>20 US states</strong> have enacted comprehensive consumer privacy laws (including California, Texas, Virginia, Colorado, and others), so if you serve a national US audience, GDPR-level practices are an increasingly prudent baseline regardless of California.</p>
